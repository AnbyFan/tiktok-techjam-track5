Where You Stand

Your patch-based CLIP ViT-L-14 + logistic regression setup already aligns with a proven research direction: recent work confirms frozen CLIP features (rather than fine-tuned ones) generalize far better across unseen generators, because a trained classifier tends to overfit to one generator's "fingerprint" and treat everything else as a sink class for "real". Getting 100% on your test set is encouraging but likely reflects in-distribution performance — the real test is unseen generators, so your next moves should prioritize generalization and robustness over squeezing more accuracy on the current test split.

Combining Patch + Platt Scaling

Platt scaling is a calibration step, not a feature extraction change, so it's complementary rather than competing with patch pooling. You can calibrate your patch-based model's decision scores with Platt scaling on top of the logistic regression output — this won't change raw accuracy but will give you well-calibrated confidence scores (important for a competition if judges evaluate log-loss, Brier score, or ROC-AUC rather than just accuracy). Practically: keep the 4×4 patch-mean-pooled logistic regression as your base classifier, then fit Platt scaling (a second logistic regression on the base model's raw score) on a held-out calibration split. This is cheap and directly stacks with what you already have.

Attention-Based Aggregation Over Mean Pooling

This is the most impactful concrete upgrade available right now. A CVPR 2026 workshop paper (TAP: Tunable Attention Pooling) shows that discarding CLIP's patch tokens in favor of only the cls token or mean pooling suppresses exactly the localized generative artifacts that matter for detection — since artifacts often appear in specific image regions rather than uniformly. Their fix: a single learnable attention-pooling layer where a trainable query vector cross-attends over all patch tokens (plus the cls token), refined through a small residual MLP, before final classification.

Results were substantial: on the OpenSDI benchmark, adding TAP to a frozen ViT-L backbone improved mean F1 from 87.52 to 91.64 and mean accuracy from 86.24% to 91.71% — a roughly 4–5 point gain purely from replacing pooling with learned attention. On the harder Chameleon in-the-wild benchmark, TAP pushed accuracy from 83.31% to 95.64% with a ViT-G backbone. For your setup, this suggests swapping mean pooling for a lightweight learned attention-pooling head (even a single multi-head attention layer with one trainable query) could meaningfully outperform your current 4×4 mean-pool approach, especially on out-of-distribution generators.

Patch Strategy Variations
Strategy	Rationale	Expected effect
Overlapping patches	Captures artifacts that fall on grid boundaries, reduces edge-cutting effects	Modest accuracy gain, higher compute
Multi-scale (e.g., 2×2 + 4×4 + 8×8)	Coarse grids catch global composition artifacts; fine grids catch texture/frequency artifacts	Better robustness across generator types (GAN vs. diffusion artifacts differ in scale)
Full patch tokens (14×14 = 196 for ViT-L/14)	Avoids manual pooling entirely; let attention learn which patches matter	Best generalization per TAP results above
Region-adaptive/saliency-weighted patches	Focus compute on likely-manipulated regions (useful for partial edits/inpainting)	Helps with inpainting/local-edit detection specifically

Since CLIP ViT-L/14 already tokenizes the image into a native patch grid, an efficient middle ground is to skip your manual 4×4 pooling and instead pull the model's own patch tokens (before any pooling) and apply attention pooling directly on those — this is essentially what TAP does and avoids information loss from your resizing/re-gridding step.
